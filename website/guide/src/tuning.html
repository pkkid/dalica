Tuning and the neutral picker

The Tuning tab's five controls are one recipe entry and one pass over the pixels: a color multiplication followed by a tone curve. This chapter gives the exact curves behind Fill Light, Highlights and Shadows, what Color Temperature does to each channel, what really happens when you click a white or gray point with the Neutral Color Picker (and when you click the wrong one), and how the two magic wands choose their values.

One entry, one pass

Everything on the Tuning tab is stored as a single operation:

tuning(fill,highlights,shadows,temp,neutral)
tuning(0.25,0,0.1,0.3,#b7bbc9)
FieldRangeDefaultStored as
Fill Light0 to 10number
Highlights0 to 0.50number
Shadows0 to 0.50number
Color Temperature-1 (cooler) to +1 (warmer)0number
Neutral colora picked colornone#rrggbb or none

The sliders store their values with at most three decimals, and the wands with two. The defaults change nothing, and a Tuning entry at its defaults renders no pass at all. Numbers out of range are brought to the nearest end when read; a NaN, a missing field or a bad color leaves the entry in the recipe, kept and skipped. The Basic Fixes tab's Fill Light slider is the same fill value of the same entry. Older recipes may hold fill_light(a), which renders exactly as tuning(a,0,0,0,none) and is kept as written. (Which Tuning entry a slider changes, and when a new one is appended, is explained in Edits are recipes.)

Rendering a Tuning entry is one pixel-only pass, on the sRGB-encoded working values (0 to 1), in two steps:

Scaling all three channels by the same ratio l' / l changes brightness without changing hue: a red stays the same red, only lighter or darker. Each channel is clamped to 0..1 afterwards, so a saturated color pushed bright can lose some saturation at the top. A pixel with luma near zero (below one millionth) is left as it is, since it has no color to scale.

Fill Light, Highlights, Shadows

Fill Light

fill(l) = l + 3 · fill · l · (1 - l)³

This curve never darkens anything, keeps black at 0 and white at 1, and always rises (its slope never drops below 1/4, so tones never swap order). The extra brightness 3 · fill · l · (1 - l)³ is largest at l = 0.25, where it adds 0.316 × fill. At full strength (fill = 1):

Luma in0.10.250.50.751
fill 0.50.2090.4080.5940.7681
fill 10.3190.5660.6880.7851

Relative to the pixel, the darker it is the more it is lifted, but never by more than 1 + 3 · fill times (4 times at fill 1). That bound is deliberate: it keeps the noise in near-black areas from being blown up. The strength 3 stays below 4, the most that would still keep the curve rising everywhere.

Highlights and Shadows

Both use one brightening curve on 0..1, applied to one half of the range:

lift(t, a) = 2t - t² - t · (1 - t)^(1 + 4a)

highlights, for l above 0.5:  out = 0.5 + 0.5 · lift((l - 0.5) / 0.5, highlights)
shadows,    for l below 0.5:  out = 0.5 - 0.5 · lift((0.5 - l) / 0.5, shadows)

At amount 0, lift is the identity. For any amount it starts with slope 1 (so it joins the untouched half without a kink at 0.5), never falls below the identity, and ends at exactly 1 (so white stays white and black stays black). Highlights therefore brightens only the tones above the middle; Shadows darkens only the tones below it, deepening the blacks. Fill Light is applied first, and Highlights and Shadows act on the filled luma.

Highlights 0.50.6 → 0.6290.75 → 0.8440.9 → 0.977
Shadows 0.50.4 → 0.3710.25 → 0.1560.1 → 0.023

Color Temperature

Despite its name, the slider does not model a light source in kelvin. It is a simple, predictable warm and cool balance on the sRGB-encoded values: three channel gains that move in straight lines with the slider value t, then normalized so that a gray keeps its luma.

g = (1 + 0.12 t,  1 + 0.03 t,  1 - 0.15 t)       for red, green, blue
gains = g / luma(g)

Warmer means more red, a little more green and less blue; cooler the reverse. Dividing by the luma of the gains is what keeps the slider from also acting as a brightness control: a neutral gray of luma 0.5 is still luma 0.5 after any temperature, it just turns slightly yellow or blue. The temperature gains are multiplied with the neutral color's gains (below) into one multiplication per channel.

The Neutral Color Picker

The picker answers one question: "what color is this thing that should be gray?" If a patch of snow, a white wall or a gray rock comes out bluish, the picture has a blue cast, and the amount of blue in that patch tells Dalíca exactly how much to remove from every pixel.

What a click does

  1. Dalíca renders, on a worker thread, the picture the Tuning entry receives: the recipe without the Tuning entry being edited, cropped, straightened and turned as the edit view shows it, with its longer side at 256 pixels.
  2. It averages a small square around the point you clicked, reaching 1 % of the picture's shorter side each way (at least one pixel). On a 256 by 205 picture that is 2 pixels each way: a 5 by 5 square of 25 pixels, about 2 % of the picture's width across, so a single noisy pixel cannot decide the result. Fully transparent pixels are left out.
  3. That average color is stored in the recipe as the entry's neutral, for example #b7bbc9.

At render time, the stored color c (scaled to 0..1) gives one gain per channel:

y = luma(c) = 0.2126 c.r + 0.7152 c.g + 0.0722 c.b
gain[k] = y / c[k]        each kept within 0.25 to 4

Multiplying the picked color by these gains turns it into the gray of its own luma: it becomes neutral without getting brighter or darker. Every other pixel in the picture is multiplied by the same three gains, so the whole picture shifts by the same correction. A picked black carries no color and changes nothing; a channel that is 0 in the picked color gets the largest gain, 4.

For the snow, y = 0.2126 × 183 + 0.7152 × 187 + 0.0722 × 201 = 187.2, so the gains are:

The correction is modest (7 % less blue), and it is enough: the snow reads as white, and everything else in the picture shifts by the same small step toward warm.

A wrong pick

The picker trusts you. Click something that is not neutral, and Dalíca will make that gray, with the same arithmetic. Picking the fox's orange side gives (144, 88, 43), luma 96.7, and gains of 0.671, 1.098 and 2.248: the fur becomes a gray of 97, and every other pixel is pushed toward blue just as hard. The snow, (183, 187, 201) before, becomes (123, 206, 251) in Dalíca's render, its blue channel at or near the top.

What is stored: the recipe keeps the picked color, not the point you clicked and not the gains. The correction is therefore fixed: if you later change an edit that comes before the Tuning (a different Auto Fix, say), the same gains still apply, and you may want to pick again.

Auto lighting and Auto color

The two magic wands on the Tuning tab do not add operations of their own. Each one measures the same 256-pixel picture the picker uses (the picture the Tuning entry receives), computes values, and applies them to the sliders as an ordinary change of the Tuning session, so they land in the same tuning(…) entry and are undone with it. The measurement runs as a background job; asking the same wand again for the same photo cancels the one before.

Auto lighting

Auto lighting reads three percentiles of the luma: the 1st (the blacks), the 50th (the median) and the 99th (the highlights), and sets each slider from one of them, rounded to hundredths:

Auto color

The Auto color wand runs Auto Color's own balance measurement (gray world and white patch combined, clamped to 1/1.35 to 1.35; see Auto Fix, Contrast and Color) and turns the gains into a neutral color. Since the picker makes a color c gray with the gains luma(c) / c, a color proportional to 1 / gains gives back the same proportions. The wand scales it so its brightest channel is 230, which keeps the ratios precise in 8 bits without touching the ceiling, and stores it as #rrggbb. When too few pixels say anything about color (fewer than 1 %, or 16), it picks nothing.

On the snowy fox, the balance gains are red 1.0022, green 1.0017, blue 0.9764, and the wand picks #e0e0e6, (224, 224, 230). As a neutral color that gives gains of 1.002, 1.002 and 0.976. Unlike the Auto Color operation, the wand takes one measurement and does not fold back its result, and the color is applied through Tuning, where you can adjust the temperature on top of it.
