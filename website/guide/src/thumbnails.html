Thumbnails

A light table cell in Dalíca is never blank, even when you drag the scroll bar through 100,000 photos at full speed. This chapter explains the three layers that make that possible, the 24-byte color sketch at the bottom of them, the packed disk cache, and how edited and rotated photos get their thumbnails.

Three layers, each drawn until the next arrives

Making a thumbnail of a 24-megapixel JPEG takes close to a tenth of a second (87 ms in release, measured for the scan job). Dragging the scroll thumb at full speed can bring a whole new screen of cells into view every frame, and a frame at 60 Hz lasts under 17 milliseconds. No amount of speed closes that gap, so Dalíca does not try: every cell always has something to draw from memory, and better versions replace it as they arrive.

1. The color sketch
A blurred preview of 24 to 26 bytes per photo, kept in the index and held in memory for every photo in the library. Any cell can be drawn from it on any frame without touching the disk.
2. The size ladder
Real thumbnails at 128, 256, 512 and 1,024 pixels on the long side, plus a screen-size preview for the edit view, in device pixels. The 128 and 256 sizes are made for every photo when it is scanned; the larger ones when something first asks for them.
3. The full decode
Only for sizes above the ladder, which in practice means zooming in the edit view.

When a cell is drawn, it asks for the smallest ladder size that covers it. If that size is in memory, it is drawn. If not, the largest smaller size in memory is scaled up (blurry, then sharp a moment later), and the missing size is queued at the visible priority. If no ladder size is in memory at all, the sketch is drawn. Only a photo that was never scanned shows the plain gray placeholder. The engine answers these questions from memory and never waits; the release budget at every review is zero gray cells while the speed-control thumb drags through a scanned library at its top speed.

The color sketch

The sketch is in the style of ThumbHash, written from the public description of the idea, with a byte layout of Dalíca's own. It is made from the photo's 128 entry right after a scan decodes the photo.

  1. Average down. The picture is averaged into at most 32 cells on its long side (weighted by alpha, in sRGB values).
  2. Split into three channels. Lightness L = (r + g + b) / 3, and two color differences, P = (r + g) / 2 − b (yellow against blue) and Q = r − g (red against green).
  3. Transform. Each channel goes through a cosine transform, and only its low-frequency corner is kept: a triangle of frequencies where cx / nx + cy / ny < 1. Lightness keeps 6 frequencies along the long side and at least 4 along the short one. The two color channels keep 4 × 4 at every aspect, because sharp color edges, such as a horizon, need them most.
  4. Quantize. Each channel stores its mean, its largest other coefficient (the scale, stored as the square root of scale over a cap, rounded up so every coefficient stays inside it), and every other coefficient as a 4-bit fraction of that scale.

The number of coefficients follows from the aspect, so the length of a valid sketch is fixed by its first two bytes: 26 bytes for a square photo, 24 for a long thin one, never more than the 32-byte cap. Across the 56 photos of this guide's set, 47 sketches came out at 24 bytes and 9 at 26. Every step uses only addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, square roots and rounding, with a cosine of Dalíca's own instead of the platform's maths library, so the same pixels give the same bytes on every operating system. Run on the guide's photo of a Kyoto shrine, the tool that made these pictures produced exactly the sketch bytes that Dalíca itself had stored in that library's index.

Decoding checks the version, the exact length, every code's range and that the padding is zero; anything else is refused as an error, never a crash. Decoding and drawing it at 32 pixels took about 5 µs in a test run for this guide, and encoding it from the 128 image about 25 µs.

What it costs, and what else it does

Measured at 100,000 photos, the sketches added 4.49 MB to the index journal and took the snapshot from 11.18 MB to 15.67 MB: about 45 bytes per photo stored, with its photo id and encoding. In memory, with its key, the sketch costs about 4.8 MB for 100,000 photos. That is cheap enough to hold every photo's sketch at all times, which is the point.

The sketch also feeds color search. A photo's color summary (what color:green matches) is read from its sketch decoded as a grid of 16 cells on its long side, so it costs no file read and follows every redrawn sketch: a photo turned black and white moves to color:bw. Decoding a sketch and reading its summary takes about 3.2 µs.

The size ladder

At scan time, one decode makes the 256 image, which is shrunk to 128, and the sketch is made from the 128. Photos then fill in over a few seconds instead of waiting on one decode per size. The 512, 1,024 and screen-size entries are made by a fetch job the first time a cell or the edit view asks for them: read from the disk cache when the entry is there, otherwise decoded from the photo (never enlarged) and stored. The screen-size preview's long side is the display's (2,560 when the display is not known yet, at most 7,680), and its cache key includes that size, so moving to another display never shows a preview made for the wrong one.

Decoded thumbnails are held in a memory cache with a fixed budget of 192 MiB, least recently used first out, whatever the size of the library. Entries are JPEG at quality 90, chosen because it is fast to decode.

The packed disk cache

Thumbnails live in Dalíca's cache folder, thumbs/ under ~/.cache/dalica on Linux, ~/Library/Caches/Dalica on macOS and %LOCALAPPDATA%\Dalica\cache on Windows. Instead of one file per thumbnail, each ladder size has one append-only pack file of entry bytes and one index of fixed-size records:

Keys

An entry's key is a 128-bit FNV-1a hash of the photo's path (with its length), its file size, its modification time in nanoseconds, a hash of the recipe it was drawn with, and the renderer version. FNV-1a is used because it gives the same value on every platform and compiler, unlike the standard library's hasher. The key is content-addressed: change any input and the old entry is simply never asked for again, so there is no cache invalidation to get wrong. A photo edited outside Dalíca gets a new size or time and therefore new thumbnails; a new version of the thumbnail renderer makes every old entry unreachable at once. The renderer version is per edit operation, so a new version of one effect remakes only the photos that use it.

Integrity, compaction and pruning

  • Checksums. Each index record carries its own CRC-32, so a torn or damaged record costs only its own entry. Each entry's bytes carry a CRC-32 that is checked the first time a process reads it; a mismatch is a miss, and the thumbnail is made again.
  • No per-entry sync. Writes append the bytes and then the record, without syncing each entry. A crash loses at most the tail, which is just thumbnails to make again.
  • Compaction. Dead entries (replaced, removed, photos that left) are counted. Once they pass 25 % of a pack (and at least 1 MiB), a background job writes the size's next generation beside the current one, the pack first and then its index; a generation counts only once its index exists, and reads go on from the old one meanwhile. That is why the files are named 128-00000001.pack: the number is the generation, as 8 hex digits.
  • Budget. The cache may use 2 GiB by default (the setting accepts 64 MiB to 1 TiB). Past it, a pruning job takes it down to 80 % of the budget, the largest sizes first (screen size, then 1,024, then 512) and the oldest entries of a size first. Entries of photos whose folder cannot be reached are stepped over, because they could not be made again until the drive returns.
  • Only its own files. The cache lists and deletes only files of its own naming shape, so pointing it at a folder with other files in it never deletes them. The folder is held with an operating-system lock; a second process gets a temporary cache of its own instead.

Why packs beat loose files

One file per thumbnail is the obvious design, and it was measured against the pack with 100,000 entries of about 6 KB each on an NVMe disk. Written, the pack took 0.94 s and the loose files 6.85 s. On disk the pack was 604 MB in 11 files and the loose files 799 MB in 100,000 (each small file wastes the rest of its last disk block). Read back, the pack was faster in every case:

An earlier run had shown slow shuffled pack reads (4.7 µs against 2.5 for loose files). That turned out to be an artifact of the benchmark: it wrote the loose files last, so under memory pressure the pack's pages were evicted first. Dropping only the pack's pages while the loose files stayed cached reproduced it exactly; measured fairly, the pack is not slower.

Embedded thumbnails and RAW previews

Most cameras put a small JPEG inside every photo's EXIF data, and RAW files carry one or more full JPEG previews. Dalíca uses them to show something real before its own thumbnail exists:

  • The embedded thumbnail. The scan's header read notes where the embedded thumbnail is. When a cell comes on screen and its photo has nothing in memory, a quick job running just above the visible priority decodes that embedded thumbnail and shows it at once, if it is big enough for the cell. The scan job's proper downscale replaces it when it lands. Up to 32 MiB of these are kept in memory while their photos' scans run. They are only used for photos without edits, since an embedded thumbnail knows nothing of your recipe.
  • RAW files. Dalíca shows a RAW file through its largest embedded preview, which is also what its thumbnails are made from.
  • Very large photos. The JPEG decoder Dalíca uses cannot decode at a reduced scale, so a JPEG is decoded in full and then resized, up to 64 megapixels. Past that, a photo is not decoded just for a thumbnail: the camera's own small copy is used if there is one, and otherwise the cell says why it cannot show the photo. A large TIFF (a stitched panorama, say) is decoded a strip or tile at a time and reduced as it goes, or uses a reduced-resolution page when the file has one that covers the size; a lossy WebP is reduced row by row at any size.

Every thumbnail is decoded with the photo's EXIF orientation applied and converted to sRGB through its ICC profile, unless View > Use Color Management is off. That option is part of the cache key, so the two kinds of entries never mix.

Edited and rotated photos

Thumbnails always show your edits. An edited photo's entries and sketch are drawn by the same renderer the edit view uses, from a decode of the photo sized to fit; when the edits keep only part of the picture (a tight crop), the photo is decoded larger in proportion, at most 4,096 pixels on its long side, so the crop's thumbnail is as sharp as an unedited one. Measured on a 24-megapixel JPEG in release, a scan took the same time with Auto Fix as its recipe as without (about 87 ms).

When a photo's recipe changes, by either undo history, a scan or the watcher, its old entries are made dead and new ones are queued at the visible priority: at once for up to 64 photos, and for the rest of a large batch edit in the background until a cell asks for them. Until the new entries land, the old images keep being drawn, so an edited photo never flashes blank.

Rotations are applied at draw time

Rotations and flips are left out of the thumbnail's pixels, and out of its key. The light table turns the picture as it paints the cell. So rotating a photo, or a hundred photos, never remakes a thumbnail: the entries of a photo whose recipe is only a quarter turn are byte for byte its unedited ones. Running the scan job on the church with the recipe rotate(90) gave a 128 entry with the same MD5 as the unedited one, and the same sketch. A recipe that holds a crop and a rotation is drawn with the crop only, then turned in the cell.

Why: rotating is the most common thing people do to a freshly imported folder, often to dozens of photos at once. Making it free keeps the light table instant and the cache free of near-duplicates.
