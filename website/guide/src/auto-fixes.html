Auto Fix, Contrast and Color

The three one-click fixes store no settings at all: each one looks at the picture every time it is drawn and decides what to do. This chapter shows exactly what they measure, the formulas and limits they apply, and why pressing a fix a second time normally changes nothing, with the numbers taken from Dalíca's own renders of real photos.

What the fixes look at

In the recipe the fixes are just autocontrast(), autocolor() and autofix(), with nothing between the parentheses. The numbers they use are worked out at render time, from the picture as it stands at their place in the recipe: after the crop and the straighten, and after every operation applied before them. That is why cropping first changes what Auto Fix does, and why an Auto Color after a Sepia sees a sepia picture.

They never measure the picture they are drawing. The renderer gives each of them its own copy of the picture at that point, rendered so that its longer side is 256 pixels (smaller pictures as they are). A 128-pixel thumbnail, the edit view and a full-resolution Save all hand the fix the same 256-pixel picture, so all three reach exactly the same black point and the same color gains. Fully transparent pixels are left out. All values are the sRGB-encoded channel values scaled to 0..1.

Each fix then becomes one pass over the pixels: per-channel gains, then the levels curve (read from a table of 4 096 steps with linear interpolation), then, for Auto Fix, a little Fill Light. When the measurement finds nothing to change, there is no pass at all and the picture is left exactly as it was.

About the numbers in this chapter: Dalíca does not report the values a fix chose. To draw the charts, the measurement was re-implemented line by line from crates/edit/src/analysis.rs, including the renderer's own resampling for the 256-pixel picture, and checked against Dalíca: applying the re-implemented gains and levels to the original gives Dalíca's full-size render to within 0.5 to 2 levels on average (out of 255), where Auto Contrast and Auto Fix move these photos' pixels by 14 to 35 levels.

Auto Contrast

Auto Contrast sets three numbers, a black point b, a white point w and a midtone gamma γ, and applies them to every channel with the same curve:

out = clamp((v - b) / (w - b), 0, 1) ^ γ

The same three numbers are used for red, green and blue (the channels are linked). Stretching each channel separately would also change the color balance, which is Auto Color's job; with one curve, a gray stays gray and the fix changes contrast only.

Black and white points

  1. For each channel, take the value below which 0.5 % of the pixels lie (the 0.5th percentile) and the value below which 99.5 % lie (the 99.5th percentile), by nearest rank.
  2. The black point is the darkest of the three 0.5th percentiles, and the white point the brightest of the three 99.5th percentiles. Taking the extreme channel means no channel loses more than 0.5 % of its pixels at either end.
  3. Both are moved half an 8-bit level further out (0.5 / 255), so the pixels exactly at the percentile keep a trace of their detail and only the ones beyond it clip.

The 4× cap

If the black and white points are less than 0.25 apart (64 levels), the range is widened about its center to exactly 0.25 before it is used. A stretch from w - b to the full range multiplies contrast by 1 / (w - b), so this caps Auto Contrast at 4 times the contrast. Without it, a nearly uniform picture (a gray wall, fog, a sheet of paper) would have its sensor noise stretched across the whole range. A completely flat picture stays flat: a gray of 0.5 comes out as 0.5.

The midtone gamma

After the linear stretch, Auto Contrast looks at the median luma of the stretched picture (luma with Rec. 709 weights, 0.2126 R + 0.7152 G + 0.0722 B, on the encoded values). It wants that median inside the band 0.4 to 0.6:

  • A median inside the band gets no gamma (γ = 1).
  • A median below it is brought up to 0.4, and one above it down to 0.6: the nearer edge, not the middle. The gamma that does that is γ = ln(target) / ln(median).
  • The gamma is kept within 0.8 to 1.25, so a very dark or very bright picture is moved only part of the way.
  • A median at or below 0.02, or at or above 0.98, gets no gamma (there is nothing in the midtones to move).

Auto Color

Auto Color multiplies each channel by its own gain to remove a color cast. It estimates the cast twice, in two classic ways, and takes the geometric mean.

Which pixels it reads

Only pixels that can say something about color: luma between 0.06 and 0.94 (not near black or white) and every channel below 0.98 (not clipped). If fewer than 1 % of the pixels qualify (or fewer than 16, on a tiny picture), Auto Color changes nothing.

The two estimates

Gray world
Assumes the scene averages to gray. The mean of each channel over the usable pixels gives the gains luma(mean) / mean[c], which would turn that mean into a gray of the same luma.
White patch
Assumes the brightest usable things are white. The 99th percentile of each channel gives the gains luma(white) / white[c].

The two are combined channel by channel as sqrt(grayworld × whitepatch), divided by the luma of the result (so the picture keeps its brightness), and each gain is clamped to 1/1.35 to 1.35 (0.741 to 1.35).

The fold-back: measuring again

One measurement is not the end. Clipping, the clamp and the changing set of usable pixels mean that a picture balanced with the first gains, measured again, may still show a little cast. So Auto Color measures the balanced picture again and folds what it still finds back into the gains, round after round:

  1. Measure the picture with the current gains applied. The result is a correction for each channel.
  2. Stop if every channel's correction is under 0.2 % (less than half an 8-bit level at white), or the channel is held at a limit and the correction asks it to go further.
  3. Otherwise multiply the correction into the gains (clamped to the limits) and go again, at most 12 rounds. Stop early if the gains would move by less than 0.05 %.

Finally, if no channel's gain differs from 1 by 1 % or more, the gains are dropped altogether. Most photos need one or two rounds. The limits always apply to the whole result, never per round, so pressing Auto Color can never push a channel past 1.35.

Auto Fix

Auto Fix is the color, the contrast and a little Fill Light, measured together on the 256-pixel picture and applied as one pass. The order of the measurement matters, because the levels magnify any color error:

  1. Color on its own. The gains are found exactly as Auto Color finds them: full corrections, until under 0.2 %.
  2. Color on the leveled picture. The gains are then measured again on the picture as Auto Fix will leave it: balanced, then leveled with levels measured afresh on that balanced picture. This is what a second press of Auto Fix would read. Here each round takes only half of the correction it finds (the correction to the power 0.5), at most 12 rounds, stopping when what is left is under 1 %. Taking half avoids overshoot: a change of the gains moves the levels, which magnify the change, and full steps could swing back and forth.
  3. Drop tiny corrections. Gains within 1 % of 1 in every channel are dropped.
  4. Levels. Auto Contrast's black point, white point and gamma, measured on the balanced picture (never stacked on earlier rounds), and dropped if they would move no value by half a level.
  5. Fill Light, only if still needed. On the balanced and leveled picture, take the median luma m. If it is below 0.4, the bottom of Auto Contrast's band, Auto Fix adds Fill Light of (0.4 - m) × 0.8, at most 0.2, through the same curve as the Tuning tab's Fill Light (see Tuning and the neutral picker). A picture whose levels already brought its midtones into the band gets none.

On the warm sand, the second phase measures twice, takes one half-step, and ends at red 0.933, green 1.013, blue 1.071, slightly further than Auto Color alone. On the dark fox, the levels leave the median at about 0.275 (the gamma limit held them back), so Auto Fix adds Fill Light of 0.10. On the hazy valley the median is in the band and the fill is 0.

Why pressing again settles

A fix is a step in the recipe, so a second press of Auto Fix measures the picture the first press made. Each part of the fix is built to find nothing to do on its own output:

  • Black and white points. After a press, the darkest 0.5 % sit at 0 and the brightest 0.5 % at 1, piled up where they were clipped. The second press finds its percentiles at the ends, so the black point is 0, the white point 1, and there is no stretch.
  • Midtones. The first press put the median luma on the edge of the band (or left it inside), so the second finds it in the band and uses no gamma.
  • Color. The first press's gains were already folded back until a new measurement found under 0.2 % (Auto Color) or 1 % (Auto Fix), measured on exactly the picture a second press reads. The second press finds less than that and drops it.
  • Fill Light. Only for a median under 0.4, and the first press left it at 0.4 or above.
  • Nothing left. Levels whose black point is under one level, whose white point is within one level of the top, and which move none of 17 sample values by half a level count as no levels. With no gains, no levels and no fill, the fix prepares no pass: the second press still records a step in the photo's history, but it renders as nothing.

When pressing again does more

The limits hold for each press: at most 4× the contrast, gains within 1/1.35 to 1.35, the gamma within 0.8 to 1.25. When one of them held the first press back, the picture still has something the fix wants to change, and the next press moves it again, again within the limits. Pressing again is how you ask for more on such a photo.

The color limit works the same way, and shows why it exists. The torii gates below are mostly vivid orange, which breaks the gray-world assumption: the photo is not a gray scene with an orange cast, it is an orange scene. The limits stop Auto Color from turning it blue in one press, but a second press asks again, and the path between the gates turns cyan.
